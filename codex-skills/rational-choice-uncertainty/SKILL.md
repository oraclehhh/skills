## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### Bounded Rationality
Use bounded rationality whenever a decision looks "irrational" but the person had limited attention, memory, time, information, or computational capacity. Do not ask only "What is the optimal answer?" Ask "What constraints shaped the judgment process?"

### Judgment as Cue Integration
Treat judgment as the use of cues to infer an uncertain criterion. In ideal judgment, cues are weighted by diagnostic value. In human judgment, salient, recent, vivid, or narratively coherent cues often receive too much weight. Use this model to audit hiring, forecasting, diagnosis, legal reasoning, and investment judgments.

### Lens Model
Use the lens model when people infer hidden states from observable cues. Separate:
- **Ecological validity**: how strongly each cue actually predicts the target.
- **Cue utilization**: how strongly the judge relies on each cue.
- **Achievement**: how well judgment matches the target.

The practical move is to compare actual cue weights with used cue weights.

### Linear Models as Judgment Baselines
Simple linear combinations often outperform unaided intuitive judgment because they are consistent, transparent, and resistant to noise. Use a linear model as a baseline before trusting holistic intuition. If humans override the model, require a specific reason tied to new valid information.

### Anchoring and Adjustment
Use this when an initial value, default, prior offer, visible number, or arbitrary reference point may be pulling judgment. The failure mode is insufficient adjustment. Countermeasure: generate an independent estimate before seeing anchors, then use ranges and outside-view base rates.

### Heuristic Judgment
Heuristics are efficient shortcuts, not merely errors. They fail when the cue that feels relevant is not statistically diagnostic.
- **Availability**: ease of recall is mistaken for frequency or likelihood.
- **Representativeness**: resemblance is mistaken for probability.
- **Simulation / scenario thinking**: ease of imagining a story is mistaken for likelihood.

### Explanation-Based Judgment
People do not merely count evidence; they build stories. In law, diagnosis, strategy, and forecasting, a coherent explanation can dominate disconfirming evidence. Use this model to ask: "What alternative story would explain the same facts?" and "What evidence would make the favored story false?"

### Randomness and Causality
Humans tend to see pattern, streak, cause, and intention in noisy data. Use this when analyzing sports streaks, market moves, performance variation, medical outcomes, or small samples. Check sample size, base rate, regression to the mean, and whether the causal mechanism is specified before naming a pattern.

### Rational Thinking Under Uncertainty
Good uncertainty reasoning requires an explicit sample space, base rates, conditional probabilities, and payoffs. Use probability trees and natural frequencies to prevent base-rate neglect and conditional-probability confusion. For diagnostic problems, always ask: prevalence, hit rate, false alarm rate, and action threshold.

### Preference Construction
Preferences are not always stable pre-existing facts; they are often constructed by framing, comparison set, elicitation method, and context. Use this to diagnose reversals between pricing, choice, ranking, and willingness-to-pay.

### From Preference to Choice
Choice is the procedure that turns values into action. Different procedures produce different choices: weighted utility, elimination by aspects, lexicographic rules, satisficing, and social or moral constraints. Ask what decision procedure the person is implicitly using.

### Rational Decision Theory
Use expected utility or expected value when probabilities and payoffs can be made explicit. Build a decision tree, assign probabilities, assign utilities, compute expected utility, then run sensitivity analysis. The goal is not false precision; it is disciplined comparison.

### Descriptive Decision Theory
Use descriptive models when actual human behavior deviates systematically from rational choice. Prospect theory, loss aversion, framing effects, sunk cost, endowment effects, regret, and status quo bias explain predictable gaps between normative and observed choice.

### Praise Uncertainty
Uncertainty is not a defect to eliminate; it is the condition under which judgment matters. The practical stance is calibrated humility: quantify what can be quantified, expose assumptions, compare alternatives, and design decisions that remain robust when estimates are wrong.
